# Offset Backup Iron Sights: Field Notes

A magnified optic or a red dot can fail at the worst time: a dead battery, a fogged lens, or a cracked emitter after a hard knock. Offset irons give you a second sighting system without removing the primary optic. Instead of sitting on top of the rail, they mount at an angle, so a quick roll of the rifle brings them into your eye line.

## Why a 45-degree offset

The 45-degree position keeps the sights clear of the scope bell and turret while staying close enough to the bore that holdovers at short range stay simple. For shooters running a low-power variable optic, this is often the cleanest way to cover close targets and a dead-optic scenario at the same time.

For a rail-mounted option, see these [45 degree offset backup iron sights from Ozark Armament](https://www.amazon.com/OZARK-ARMAMENT-Degree-Offset-Backup/dp/B071ZF1DS2/), a front and rear pair that mounts on standard Picatinny rail.

## Setup checklist

- Mount the rear sight far enough back for a full sight picture with your normal cheek weld after the cant.
- Keep the front sight forward of the optic objective so it does not clip the field of view.
- Zero at 25 or 50 yards with the rifle canted the way you will actually use it.
- Practice the roll-over drill dry before live fire so the transition becomes automatic.

## Maintenance

Check mounting screws after the first range session, add a small amount of medium threadlocker if needed, and confirm zero after any transport in a hard case. Offset sights take more side impact than top-mounted sights, so inspect them whenever the rifle is dropped or bumped.
